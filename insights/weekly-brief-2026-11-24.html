Weekly Brief: the ODPC's compensation question, Equinor's IT attack, and an old NAS flaw

Published: 24 November 2026 Read time: 7 min Author: RETACH

Three stories for organisations in Kenya and East Africa this week, what each means in practice, and what to do about it. One tests how data protection enforcement works. One shows what happens when an energy company's business systems are attacked. One is a reminder that old flaws keep working. All sources are public and linked at the end.

  1. The ODPC closes the BRS case, and a court questions its compensation power
  2. Equinor's IT attack: production ran, but business systems did not
  3. A flaw patched in 2024 is now being used against NAS devices

GRC · Kenya · Data protection

The ODPC closes the BRS case, and a court questions its compensation power

On 17 November, TechTrendsKE reported that the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner (ODPC) had closed its investigation into the exposure of Business Registration Services (BRS) records without issuing a penalty notice. The outgoing Data Commissioner, Immaculate Kassait, said the office opened the case on its own initiative and issued an enforcement notice. It did not move on to a penalty notice because BRS carried out what the enforcement notice required. She also said no affected person had asked for compensation. The "more than two million businesses" scale in coverage dates from the 2025 incident, and we found no ODPC statement giving it.

Separately, Business Daily and TechTrendsKE reported on 15 November that Justice Francis Andayi of the High Court set aside a KSh 1.25 million compensation award the ODPC had made against a Nairobi healthcare business, in a complaint about a CCTV camera in an examination room. According to those reports, the judge said section 65 of the Data Protection Act gives a person a right to compensation but does not expressly give the Commissioner the job of deciding it, and he raised fair-hearing concerns. The reports do not say the ruling struck down the regulation or cancelled earlier awards.

On 9 November the President nominated Lydia Sitienei Chelagat, the Communications Authority's director of legal services, to succeed Ms Kassait. We read press reports, not the judgment, and they do not give its date.

Why it matters here: In the BRS case the sequence was an enforcement notice first, with a penalty notice only if it is not carried out, so acting on a notice is the part an organisation controls. What the court questioned is who decides compensation, not whether section 65 gives a right to it.

RETACH's reading: the ruling is not a reason to treat data protection as lower risk. A dated record of what you changed after an incident or notice is something you can build now.

What to do this week

  • Run any regulator notice as a projectName an owner and a deadline, and list each corrective step the notice requires.
  • Keep dated proof of every fixConfiguration changes, access reviews and test results, saved in one place.
  • Take advice before leaning on the rulingIt concerns one case. If you have a compensation order, ask your advocate whether and how to challenge it.
  • Map registry-style personal dataAddresses, phone numbers, ownership details: where you hold them, and who and what can query them.

General information, not legal advice. For decisions about your own obligations, consult a qualified advocate.

Critical infrastructure · Energy

Equinor's IT attack: production ran, but business systems did not

Equinor, the Norwegian energy company, said on 17 November that it had detected an ongoing cyber-attack on some IT systems. Its latest update, on 20 November, says the attack has been stopped, production ran as normal, and there is no indication that data was stolen or altered. The company has reported the incident to the police and said earlier that the attack did not appear to target Equinor specifically. Its statement does not say how the attackers got in or which systems were hit.

Separately, NIST published an initial public draft of SP 800-82 Revision 4, its guide to operational technology (OT) security, on 21 September, with comments open until 30 November. NIST says the draft is organised around its Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 and adds more on asset management, network monitoring and zero trust architecture. It is a draft, not final guidance. An FBI and CISA fact sheet of 23 September urges operators to limit what third-party integrators can reach.

Why it matters here: Energy operators, and anyone with plant or field systems, rely on corporate IT for scheduling, maintenance, supplier orders and email, even where control systems sit apart.

RETACH's reading: Equinor's statement shows a company can lose some business systems and still keep production running. Whether you can depends on how well IT is separated from operations and whether you have rehearsed without it. The statement does not say how Equinor managed it.

What to do this week

  • List the systems operations lean onScheduling, maintenance, email, supplier portals. For each, write what happens if it is down for two days.
  • Map where IT meets operationsWhich links exist, who can cross them, and whether crossings are logged.
  • Review integrator and vendor accessThe FBI and CISA fact sheet recommends monitored, on-demand remote access and the minimum rights needed.
  • Rehearse a manual fallbackAgree how teams communicate and record work if email and corporate systems are unavailable.
  • If you run OT, read the NIST draftComments close on 30 November.

Practical · Patching · Small organisations

A flaw patched in 2024 is now being used against NAS devices

On 29 October, Synology said attackers are exploiting a vulnerability patched in December 2024 against devices running outdated DiskStation Manager (DSM) software, with the aim of encrypting data and demanding a ransom. The statement did not name the flaw. The Hacker News reported on 3 November that it matches Synology advisory SA-24:20 (5 November 2024), which rates CVE-2024-10441 critical (CVSS 9.8), allows remote code execution and lists no mitigation other than updating. The Hacker News reported on 19 November that CISA added the flaw to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 18 November. We could not load CISA's own page, so that date rests on the press report.

The same CISA update, per The Hacker News, included CVE-2026-90436 in Apache Axis 1.x. Rapid7 says Apache disclosed it on 6 November, that Axis 1.x is end-of-life with no fixed release planned, and that it detected exploitation against Atlassian Confluence Data Center on 13 November where a non-default Remote API setting was enabled. One flaw has had a fix for two years; the other will not get one. Synology gives no number of affected devices.

Why it matters here: The Communications Authority's National KE-CIRT report for April to June 2026, published in July 2026, lists weak configurations, delayed patching, insecure cloud setups and inadequate authentication among the common causes of successful cyber incidents in Kenya. It also advises organisations to keep offline backups.

RETACH's reading: a NAS often holds a small organisation's file shares and its backups, so one unpatched box can put both at risk.

What to do this week

  • Find every NAS, firewall and applianceRecord the model, software version and who is responsible for updates.
  • Check DSM against the advisoryUpdate to the fixed build or later, and turn on automatic updates, which Synology recommends.
  • Hold a backup the NAS cannot reachAt least one copy offline or otherwise out of reach of the NAS's credentials.
  • List unsupported softwareFor each item, decide to replace, isolate or accept the risk in writing. If you run Confluence Data Center, check the Remote API setting.
