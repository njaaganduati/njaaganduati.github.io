Weekly Brief: bank cyber risk, a draft OT security guide, and Kenya's patching message

Published: 10 November 2026 Read time: 7 min Author: RETACH

Three stories for organisations in Kenya and East Africa, what each means in practice, and what to do about it. This is a thin week for fresh headlines, so the brief leans on three recent official publications that most readers will not have opened: the Central Bank of Kenya's supervision report, a US draft guide to operational technology security that is open for comment, and the Communications Authority's quarterly threat report. Every source is public and linked at the end.

Regional · Banking supervision

What the CBK's report says about cyber risk and technology suppliers

The Central Bank of Kenya's Bank Supervision Annual Report 2025 covers the year to 31 December 2025. The CBK posted it on its website on 22 September 2026. In its section on innovation risk, the CBK reports that 82% of commercial banks and 93% of microfinance banks ranked cyber risk as the top risk from innovation. A separate CBK survey on technology service providers found that 38 of 52 respondents named cybersecurity and data privacy as the biggest challenge in working with those providers. Of the respondents, 83% said they have a formal incident response plan for a breach at a provider.

The report also restates the CBK's 2017 cybersecurity guidance, under which banks must tell the CBK about a cyber incident within 24 hours. It says a March 2025 survey fed into a planned update of that guidance, and that phishing, ransomware and DDoS attacks are rising.

What the report does not give: incident counts, loss amounts, or the number of respondents behind the 82% and 93% figures. RETACH has not seen those figures in the sections it reviewed.

Why it matters here: RETACH's reading: the CBK regulates banks and microfinance banks, not SACCOs, and this report sets no new duty for anyone else. But SACCOs, SMEs and county bodies often use the same kinds of technology providers, and the CBK's two worries, cyber risk and supplier dependence, are not specific to banks. The 24-hour notice period is also a useful benchmark for your own internal escalation rules. General information, not legal advice.

What to do this week

  • List your technology providersName each supplier that holds your data or has remote access to your systems, and who owns that relationship internally.
  • Check the contractDoes it say how fast the supplier must tell you about an incident? If it is silent, add it at the next renewal.
  • Write your own escalation clockDecide who must be told, and by when, once staff suspect a cyber incident. Use the CBK's 24 hours as a reference point.
  • Ask for the planAsk your top three suppliers for their incident response plan, and read it.

Critical infrastructure · OT

A draft OT security guide, open for comment until 30 November

On 21 September 2026 the US National Institute of Standards and Technology published an initial public draft of SP 800-82 Revision 4, the Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security. NIST says comments are open through 30 November 2026. The draft widens its coverage to include water and wastewater, food and agriculture, freight rail, maritime vessels and building automation systems. It is reorganised around NIST CSF 2.0, and it adds more guidance on asset management, network monitoring, zero trust, and the mixing of industrial systems with cloud and internet-connected devices.

Closer to home, the Communications Authority's report for April to June 2026 lists 545,956 system-attack events under "ICS", meaning industrial control systems. The report does not explain how an event is counted, which sectors were hit, or how many industrial devices sit behind the number.

Why it matters here: RETACH's reading: NIST guidance is American and is not a Kenyan legal requirement. Its value is as a structured reference for operators of water, power, transport and building systems, which often run old equipment that was never meant to be reachable from the internet. The Authority's figure shows that industrial systems appear in national threat counts, though it does not show how serious those events were.

What to do this week

  • Make an asset listWrite down every controller, sensor network and building system, and who maintains it.
  • Find the internet pathsCheck which of those systems can be reached from the office network, a supplier link or the internet.
  • Separate the networksConfirm that office systems and industrial systems are not on one flat network.
  • Read the draftThe NIST page links the draft and a comment template. If you run industrial systems, a short comment is open to anyone until 30 November.

Practical takeaway · Patching

Kenya's threat report, and what it tells you to patch

The Communications Authority's National KE-CIRT/CC publishes a quarterly cybersecurity report. The edition for April to June 2026 appears to have been uploaded in July 2026; the report itself carries no date. It counts 2,355,938,192 cyber threat events for the quarter, 30.03% fewer than January to March 2026. System attacks made up 2,254,287,819 of them, about 96%. Web application attacks rose 43.68% on the previous quarter, to 17,406,495.

Its recommendations are plain: apply patches, use strong passwords and multi-factor authentication, configure firewalls properly, and upgrade software the vendor no longer supports. Microsoft normally releases its monthly security updates on the second Tuesday of the month; this month, that is Tuesday 10 November.

The report gives no explanation of how events are collected, whether they are deduplicated, or how they differ from "advisories". It has no annual total and no figures on mobile money. A newer edition, for July to September 2026, has since been released. News reports of it (Capital FM, Techweez) put that quarter's total at about 2.7 billion events, up 14.45% on April to June, so the fall shown here did not last. RETACH could not open the newer report itself, so this brief uses the April to June figures. Treat the numbers as a snapshot of detected activity, not a count of successful attacks.

Why it matters here: RETACH's reading: counts this large mostly show automated scanning, and automated scanning succeeds against systems that are unpatched, exposed or protected by a weak password. That is something an SME or a county IT team can fix without a large budget.

What to do this week

  • List what faces the internetWebsites, VPNs, mail, remote desktop, cloud consoles. If it is not on the list, nobody patches it.
  • Apply this month's updatesStart with the internet-facing list. Set a date by which the rest is done, and write it down.
  • Turn on MFAStart with admin accounts, email and remote access.
  • Retire unsupported softwareMake a list of anything the vendor no longer updates, with a plan and date to replace or isolate it.
  • Review firewall rulesRemove rules nobody can explain.
