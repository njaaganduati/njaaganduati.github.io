Weekly Brief: the ODPC changes hands, Maersk's inherited-system breach, and four exploited flaws

Published: 17 November 2026 Read time: 8 min Author: RETACH

Three stories that matter to organisations in Kenya and East Africa this week, what each means in practice, and what to do about it: the handover at the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner, a breach at a shipping giant that began in an inherited system, and four software flaws that attackers are already using. GRC · Kenya · Regulator

The ODPC changes hands: what stays the same for your organisation

Techweez reported on 9 November 2026 that President William Ruto had nominated Lydia Sitienei Chelagat as Data Commissioner. It says she previously served as Director of Legal Services and Corporation Secretary at the Communications Authority. She would succeed Immaculate Kassait, whose single six-year term began in November 2020 and cannot be renewed. KBC reported on 13 November that Ms Kassait was due to leave office on 16 November. Kenya Today reported on 14 November, citing a notice from the Clerk of the National Assembly, that the committee on Communication, Information and Innovation will vet the nominee on 25 November, and that written statements on oath from the public are due by 5 pm on 24 November.

The law does not change with the person. Techweez describes the office's powers as enforcement notices and penalties of up to KES 5 million or 1% of annual turnover, whichever is lower. A decision from earlier this year shows what the regulator expects when a complaint arrives. In a decision dated 14 April, reported by TechCabal (the article carries no publication date), the ODPC found that LOLC Microfinance Bank had published a former employee's data without consent or a lawful basis. It then recommended prosecuting the bank's directors for obstruction, because the bank did not answer the regulator's notice. TechCabal reports that the offence carries a fine of up to KES 5 million, up to two years in prison, or both, and that the bank could appeal to the High Court. The report does not say whether prosecutors have acted.

Why it matters here: RETACH's reading: a change of commissioner is not a change of rules, and none of these sources says the office's priorities will shift. The practical risk is administrative. A notice from the regulator that nobody owns, or nobody answers, can turn a small complaint into a larger problem.

What to do this week

  • Name an owner for ODPC correspondenceUse a shared mailbox, a named owner and a deputy. Log every notice and every reply.
  • Check what you publishNames, photos and notices about staff, former staff or customers need a lawful basis. Confirm you have one and can show it.
  • Keep an evidence fileData map, lawful-basis record, privacy notice, processor contracts, incident procedure and training records in one place.
  • Brief your directorsIn the LOLC case the recommendation was aimed at directors. They should know that ignoring a regulator's notice is itself a risk.

General information, not legal advice. For decisions about your own obligations, consult a qualified advocate.

Maersk's breach began in a system it inherited

On 11 November 2026 A.P. Moller – Maersk said it had identified unauthorised access and data extraction from a local legacy system that came with a previous acquisition. The company says its findings so far strongly suggest no sensitive data has been disclosed. It says the incident is isolated to that system, that it has found no sign of impact on other Maersk systems or on customer and partner systems connected through APIs and EDI, and that it does not expect disruption to customers. Its investigation is continuing.

SecurityWeek reported on 12 November that a group calling itself WhiteArmor listed Maersk on its leak site on 7 November and demands USD 5 million. The group claims about 100 GB of data from a platform associated with LF Logistics, a Hong Kong company Maersk acquired in 2022. SecurityWeek notes that the group's claims have not been independently verified. Maersk has not confirmed the group's involvement, what type of data was taken, when the intrusion happened or how the attackers got in, and Maersk's statement does not name the acquired business.

Why it matters here: RETACH's reading: the lesson is not about one company. Any organisation that has merged, taken over a function or kept an old system running "for now" has the same blind spot: a system outside the main security programme, still holding real data. County bodies, SACCOs and growing SMEs all inherit systems this way.

What to do this week

  • List what you inheritedSystems that came from a merger, a takeover, a finished project or a departed supplier. Record the owner, the data held and whether it faces the internet.
  • Bring them under normal controlsSame logging, patching and access rules as the main estate, or a dated plan to retire them.
  • Check who can reach themShared admin accounts, remote access and third-party links are the usual routes in.
  • Prepare for extortion claimsDecide in advance who verifies a leak-site claim, who speaks for the organisation and who tells customers. Treat the group's own figures as unconfirmed.

Four exploited flaws, and the software your IT team may not know about

On 9 November 2026 CISA added four vulnerabilities to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalogue, citing evidence of active exploitation: GPUStack, SignalWire FreeSWITCH, OpenSSH and IBM Aspera Faspex. The 12 November deadline applies to US federal civilian agencies, though CISA encourages all organisations to prioritise KEV entries. CISA does not say who is exploiting the flaws or how many organisations are affected.

The vendors' own notices give the detail. GPUStack, an AI model-serving tool, is fixed in version 2.2.0. Its advisory of 31 July 2026 reports exploitation against a 2.1.1 deployment and warns that attackers can create persistent admin API keys, so upgrading alone does not remove their access. FreeSWITCH, a phone-system platform, is affected up to 1.11.2 and fixed in 1.11.3, according to an advisory of 28 October 2026 that also reports exploitation. IBM's bulletin of 7 November 2026 covers Aspera Faspex 5.0.0 to 5.0.19, fixed in 5.0.19 PL1, with no workaround. OpenSSH 10.6p2, released on 5 November 2026, includes a fix for a password-authentication bypass that affects some Linux systems built and run without PAM, using musl libc. OpenSSH says the root cause lies outside sshd.

Why it matters here: RETACH's reading: three of the four are products that a single team can install without IT being involved: an AI model server, a phone platform and a file-transfer server. A KEV listing settles which flaws matter. It cannot tell you what you run. That is the part only you can check.

What to do this week

  • Find the productsSearch your asset list and network for GPUStack, FreeSWITCH, Aspera Faspex and Linux servers running OpenSSH. Ask data, telephony and project teams what they have installed.
  • Compare versionsGPUStack 2.2.0, FreeSWITCH 1.11.3, Faspex 5.0.19 PL1 and OpenSSH 10.6p2 are the fixed versions named above. Follow each vendor's instructions.
  • Review access after patchingFor GPUStack and Faspex, check admin accounts and API keys. Revoke any you do not recognise.
  • Check your Linux buildsNot every OpenSSH install is affected. Confirm whether any servers use musl libc without PAM; Alpine Linux is one example, according to eSecurity Planet.
  • Make KEV a weekly habitCheck new entries against your internet-facing software every week, not once a quarter.
